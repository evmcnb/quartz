Congenital condition where bile ducts are narrowed or absent. Bile cannot be excreted so conjugated bilirubin builds up causing jaundice


Typically presents within the first weeks of life and **obstructive jaundice causes:**
- yellowing, of sclera and skin
- pale stools
- dark urine
- appetite and growth disturbances 


> [!tip] Obstructive vs physiological jaundice
> Physiological jaundice is normal from 2-7 days. Its caused by the breakdown of RBCs releasing unconjugated bilirubin.
> - Biliary atresia causes persistent jaundice (more than 14 days in term babies and 21 days in premature babies)
> - Biliary atresia causes raised conjugated bilirubin vs unconjugated

**Investigations**
- Cholangiography for definative diagnosis
- Serum bilirubin including differentiation into conjugated and total bilirubin: Total bilirubin may be normal, whereas conjugated bilirubin is abnormally high
- Liver function tests (LFTs) including serum bile acids and aminotransferases are usually raised but cannot differentiate between biliary atresia and other causes of neonatal cholestasis
- Other investigations to rule out other causes of neonatal jaundice - [[Full/Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency|Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency]], [[Full/Cystic Fibrosis|Cystic Fibrosis]]

**Management**
- Surgical only definitive treatment option - **_Kasai portoenterostomy_** procedure involves removing the **_bile ducts_** and attaching a section of the **_small intestine_** to the **_liver_** where the bile duct was attached
- Often a liver transplant is required if too much damage has occured.
