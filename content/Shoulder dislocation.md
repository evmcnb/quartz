Most common joint in the body to dislocate. Anterior shoulder dislocation >95% of all cases. 

Posterior dislocations associated with electric shocks and seizures

Shortly after the dislocation the muscles will go into spasm and tighten around the joint.
- They will hold arm against body
- Deltoid will appear flattened
- Important to assess for complications:
	- Fractures
	- Vascular damage - absent pulses, prolonged cap refill
	- Nerve damage - loss of sensation in regimental badge area


> [!tldr] Axillary nerve damage
> - C5 & C6 nerve roots. 
> - Associated with anterior dislocation
> - Loss of sensation across the lateral deltoid (regimental badge area)
> - Motor weakness in deltoid and teres minor 


### Apprehension test
![[z_attachments/Pasted image 20261016130550.png|615]]
It is likely to be positive after previous **_anterior dislocation_** or **_subluxation_** of the shoulder. This may be performed after recovery from any acute injuries.

As the arm approaches 90 degrees of external rotation, patients with shoulder instability will become anxious and apprehensive, worried that the shoulder will dislocate. There is no pain associated with the movement, only apprehension.


## Reducing shoulders


If the dislocation is recent then reduction may be attempted without any analgesia/sedation. However, other patients may require analgesia +/- sedation to ensure the rotator cuff muscles are relaxed.

Kocher's method (risk of fractures):

![[z_attachments/Pasted image 20261016130946.png|359]]

A sequence of movements with the elbow flexed to 90°:
1. Traction along the line of the humerus
2. External rotation of the arm (slowly, allowing muscle spasm to relax)
3. Adduction - bringing the elbow across toward the midline/chest
4. Internal rotation - hand moves toward the opposite shoulder, and the head usually relocates

Modified Kocher's - the external-rotation step done very gently without heavy traction.
