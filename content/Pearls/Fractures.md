## Hand Fractures

#### Scaphoid fractures  

Scaphoid fractures are the commonest carpal fractures.
1. Point of maximal tenderness over the anatomical snuffbox
2. Wrist joint effusion - Hyperacute injuries (<4hrs old), and delayed presentations (>4days old) may not present with joint effusions.
3. Pain elicited by telescoping of the thumb (pain on longitudinal compression)
4. Tenderness of the scaphoid tubercle (on the volar aspect of the wrist)
5. Pain on ulnar deviation of the wrist


- Surface of scaphoid is covered by articular cartilage with small area available for blood vessels (fracture risks avascular necrosis)
- Forms floor of anatomical snuffbox (pain on palpation)
- Risk of fracture associated with fall onto outstretched hand (tubercle, waist, or proximal 1/3)
- Ulnar deviation AP needed for visualization of scaphoid


Immobilization of scaphoid fractures difficult. Undisplaced fractures of the scaphoid waist **cast for 6-8 weeks**. Anything else requires surgical fixation

## Upper Limb Fracutres

#### Colles' fracture

- Fall onto extended outstretched hands (FOOSH)
- Described as a dinner fork type deformity
- Classical Colles' fractures have the following 3 features:

Features of the injury
1. Transverse fracture of the radius
2. 1 inch proximal to the radio-carpal joint
3. Dorsal displacement and angulation
![[z_attachments/Pasted image 20261011175324.png|490]]

#### Smith's fracture (reverse Colles' fracture)  

- Volar angulation of distal radius fragment (Garden spade deformity)
- Caused by falling backwards onto the palm of an outstretched hand or falling with wrists flexed
![[z_attachments/Pasted image 20261011180432.png|159]]
### Monteggia vs Galeazzi vs Barton's

|                         | **Monteggia**                                                                   | **Galeazzi**                                                            | **Barton’s**                                                                    |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Fracture**            | Proximal ulna (shaft, usually proximal third)                                   | Distal radial shaft (junction of middle and distal thirds)              | Distal radius, **intra-articular**, through the dorsal or volar rim             |
| **Dislocation**         | **Radial head** at the proximal radioulnar joint (elbow)                        | **Distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ)** (wrist)                              | **Radiocarpal** subluxation/dislocation: the carpus moves with the rim fragment |
| **Mechanism**           | FOOSH with forced pronation, or a direct blow to the ulna                       | FOOSH, often with pronation                                             | FOOSH or shear force across the wrist                                           |
| **Key nerve risk**      | Posterior interosseous nerve (finger/thumb extension weakness, no sensory loss) | Less typical                                                            | Median nerve (especially volar type)                                            |
| **Classification**      | Bado types I–IV (type I, anterior radial head dislocation, most common)         | —                                                                       | Volar (more common) vs dorsal                                                   |
| **Management (adults)** | ORIF of the ulna, which usually reduces the radial head                         | ORIF of the radius (“fracture of necessity”), then check DRUJ stability | ORIF, typically a buttress plate, as the fracture is unstable                   |

#### Radial head fracture

- Fracture of the radial head is common in young adults.
- It is usually caused by a fall on the outstretched hand.
- On examination, there is marked local tenderness over the head of the radius, impaired movements at the elbow, and a sharp pain at the lateral side of the elbow at the extremes of rotation (pronation and supination).

## Rib Fracture

- they are common in polytrauma with chest injuries being present in 25% of major trauma
- spontaneous rib fractures can occur rarely following coughing or sneezing: usually there is a past medical history of [[Full/osteoporosis|osteoporosis]], steroid use or [[Full/COPD|COPD]]

> [!important] Flail chest:  
> 
> - this is a serious consequence of multiple rib fractures that can occur following trauma
> - it is caused by two or more rib fractures along three or more consecutive ribs, usually anteriorly
> - the flail segment moves paradoxically during respiration and impairs ventilation of the lung on the side of injury
> - the segment can cause serious contusional injury to the underlying lung if left untreated
> - often requires treatment with invasive ventilation and surgical fixation to prevent complications

CT to diagnose, maybe CXR before if not trauma scanning. 

Management mostly conservative with:
- analgesia - ensure breathing is not affected by pain (inadequate ventilation $\rightarrow$ chest infections)
- consider nerve blocks to achieve this

Surgical fixation can be considered to manage pain if this is still an issue and the fractures have failed to heal following 12 weeks of conservative management

Flail chest segments are the only form of rib fractures which should be urgently discussed with cardiothoracic surgery as they can impair ventilation and result in significant lung trauma
## Hip Fracture

The hip is a common site of fracture, especially in osteoporotic, elderly females. The blood supply to the femoral head runs up the neck and thus avascular necrosis is a risk in displaced fractures.  

![[z_attachments/Pasted image 20261011185155.png|399]]

Features  
- pain
- the classic signs are a shortened and externally rotated leg
- patients with non-displaced or incomplete neck of femur fractures may be able to weight bear

  
The Garden system is one classification system in common use.  
- Type I: Stable fracture with impaction in valgus
- Type II: Complete fracture but undisplaced
- Type III: Displaced fracture, usually rotated and angulated, but still has boney contact
- Type IV: Complete boney disruption

#### Intracapsular hip fracture 

![[z_attachments/Pasted image 20261011185212.png]]
- **Undisplaced**: internal fixation or hemiarthroplasty if unfit
- **Displaced**: arthroplasty or hemiarthroplasty

####  Extracapsular hip fracture


	Management  
- stable intertrochanteric fractures: dynamic hip screw
- if reverse oblique, transverse or subtrochanteric fractures: intramedullary device

## Ankle fractures

Ankle fractures involve the **_lateral malleolus_** (**_distal fibula_**) or the **_medial malleolus_** (**_distal tibia_**).

The weber classification can be used to describe the type of **lateral malleolus** fracture. The focal point of the classification system is the **tibiofibular syndesmosis** - a fibrous joint. 
- **_Type A_** – below the ankle joint – will leave the syndesmosis intact
- **_Type B_** – at the level of the ankle joint – the syndesmosis will be intact or partially torn 
- **_Type C_** – above the ankle joint – the syndesmosis will be disrupted


> [!success] Ottawa Ankle Rules
> The Ottawa Rules with for ankle x-rays have a sensitivity approaching 100%
> An ankle x-ray is required only if there is any pain in the malleolar zone and any one of the following findings:  
> - bony tenderness at the lateral malleolar zone (from the tip of the lateral malleolus to include the lower 6 cm of posterior border of the fibular)
> - bony tenderness at the medial malleolar zone (from the tip of the medial malleolus to the lower 6 cm of the posterior border of the tibia)
> - inability to walk four weight bearing steps immediately after the injury and in the emergency department
> 

## Foot

#### Metatarsal fracture

Relatively common. When a fracture occurs due to repeated mechanical stress, this is termed a stress fracture. 
- The most common site of metatarsal stress fractures is the 2nd metatarsal shaft
- Occur in otherwise healthy athletes
