Most neurologists now start antiepileptics following a second epileptic seizure. NICE guidelines suggest starting antiepileptics after the first seizure if any of the following are present:  
- the patient has a neurological deficit
- brain imaging shows a structural abnormality
- the EEG shows unequivocal epileptic activity
- the patient or their family or carers consider the risk of having a further seizure unacceptable


Generalised seizures:
- Seizure onset within and rapidly engaging bilaterally distributed networks with loss of consciousness 
- Tonic-clonic, tonic, atonic, myoclonic and absence

Focal seizures:
- Seizure origin within the network limited to one cerebral hemisphere
- Most commonly arising from temporal and frontal lobes

Levofloxacin (quinolones) lowers the seizure **threshold**


> [!danger] Sodium Valproate
> _Valproate must not be started for the first time in people (male or female) younger than 55 years, unless 2 specialists independently consider and document that there is no other effective or tolerated treatment, or there are compelling reasons that the reproductive risks do not apply._


### Non-pharmaceutical management 

Urgently refer all people with suspected first seizure for assessment

- Stop driving while waiting to see the specialist for confirmation of diagnosis =
- Avoid dangerous work or leisure activities
- Mind of safety at home
- Lifestyle factors that may reduce seizure threshold - e.g. sleep deprivation, alcohol and recreational drug use

#### Contraception

Women using enzyme inducing anti-epileptic drugs **should not** use:
- oral contraceptives
- contraceptive patches or rings
- the contraceptive implant 
The enzyme inducing drugs can reduce the effectiveness of these methods. The contraceptive injection and intra-uterine devices are fine.

## Acute Management

PC setting
- Time the duration of the seizure
- Protect them from injury
- Do not restrain
- Check airway when seizure stops & recovery position
- Ambulance if first seizure, reoccurrence shortly afterward, airway issues, or other injury

### Status epilepticus

Status epilepticus is defined as:  
- a single seizure lasting > 5 minutes, or
- $\geq$ 2 seizures within a 5-minute period without the person returning to normal between them

#### Management

**ABC**
- airway adjunct
- oxygen
- blood glucose

**First-line termination of seizure**
- Pre-hospital/community - PR diazepam or buccal diazepam/midazolam 10-20mg for an adult 
- Hospital, IV lorazepam 4mg with repeat dose after 5-10 minutes as required. 

**Continuous/established status epilepticus**

After benzodiazepines fail to terminate seizures within 5-20 minutes, second-line AEDs are administered to prevent progression to refractory SE

5 min: benzo $\rightarrow$ +5 mins: benzo $\rightarrow$ +5 min: keppra (levetiracetam) $\rightarrow$ 10min: phenytoin

- **Levetiracetam:**
    - Dose: 60 mg/kg IV (max 4500 mg) over 15 minutes.
    - Advantages: Rapid administration, minimal drug interactions, good safety profile.
    - Mechanism: Modulates synaptic vesicle protein SV2A, reducing neurotransmitter release.
- **Phenytoin:**
    - Dose: Phenytoin 20 mg/kg IV at max rate 50 mg/min; fosphenytoin preferred for faster infusion and less local toxicity.
    - Side effects: Cardiac arrhythmias, hypotension; requires cardiac monitoring during infusion.
    - Mechanism: Blocks voltage-gated sodium channels stabilising neuronal membranes.
- **Sodium Valproate:**
    - Dose: 20-40 mg/kg IV over 10 minutes.
    - Useful in absence or myoclonic SE and when phenytoin contraindicated.
    - Side effects: Hepatotoxicity risk; avoid in liver disease or mitochondrial disorders.

**Refractory Status**
If no reponse within 45 minutes from onset - induction of general anaesthesia or phenobarbital is needed 


## Stepping Down Treatment

Seizure free for **over 2 years** with medications being tapered down over **2-3 months**
